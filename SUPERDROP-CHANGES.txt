FINISHED FEATURES
====================================================================

1) Bottom sheet for sending
   Sending from the share menu opens a bottom sheet instead of dragging you into
   the app. Shows icons for the devices you can tap to choose who to send to. No
   more being forced all the way into the app just to send or receive something —
   just like the old Quick Share. A much better improvement.

   Optional polish (can be kept or left out): the sheet has an entrance — it slides
   up from below the bottom of the screen with an iPhone-style variable speed (quick
   to set off, then easing smoothly into place) and, as it lands, the rounded card's
   top edge gives one small elastic "extend and settle". The bounce flows straight out
   of the slide as one continuous motion (not a separate beat afterwards). Only the
   rounded card background stretches; the contents keep their exact size, so nothing
   distorts — the device-name pill and the device picker ride up glued to the
   stretching top edge, while the bottom action row (the "Can't find the device?" link
   and the Cancel button) stays planted, so the stretch opens in the gap between them.
   And if you want, the discovered-device icons can be held back until the entrance
   finishes, so none pop in mid-slide. The bounce and the icon delay are self-contained
   and don't touch discovery — each can be kept or dropped independently of the
   bottom-sheet change.

   Technical: the slide is a view-level translate with an iOS-style cubic-bezier
   ease-out, kicked from Activity.onEnterAnimationComplete() — i.e. AFTER the activity
   window has finished its own open transition — so it runs in the already-visible
   window and isn't masked by the window animation (starting it earlier, in onCreate,
   is what made the sheet "appear already settled" with only a fade on OnePlus). The
   window's own open animation is a soft fade with no vertical translate, so it doesn't
   compete with the slide. The bounce stretches only the card background (sheet scaleY
   about the bottom) on an asymmetric profile (quick extend, slow elastic settle, no
   wobble); the content wrapper is counter-scaled about its TOP so the pill + picker
   ride up with the top edge, and the bottom action row is translated back down by the
   same amount to stay planted. It is kicked just before the slide lands so the slide
   and bounce read as one motion.

   Image for the PR: docs/pr-images/send-sheet-device.jpg (real device — OnePlus
   Nord N30 5G: header pill with your phone name, "1 file 240.5 KB", "Tap a device
   to send", a circular peer icon "Mike's phone", the QR button top-right, and
   "Can't find the device?"). Alt/emulator shot: docs/pr-images/send-bottom-sheet.png

2) Quick tile that opens a bottom sheet to receive
   A Quick Settings tile that opens the receive sheet and makes you visible to
   any nearby phone, so they can send to you straight away — no opening the app
   and digging for a button. Tapping the tile also turns on Wi-Fi and Bluetooth
   for you (and restores them when you close the sheet) and closes the
   notification shade so you can see your screen.

   Technical: a TileService whose tap forces the receiver "visible" override on,
   starts the foreground receiver service with ACTION_TILE_WAKE (which calls the
   radio-helper to enable Wi-Fi + BT), and uses startActivityAndCollapse to drop
   the shade. The prior visibility + radio state is captured and restored when the
   receive sheet closes.

   Image for the PR: docs/pr-images/receive-tile-sheet.png

3) Tap to share
   You can now tap two phones together to start a transfer. The tap passes your
   identity and your Wi-Fi address and starts the transfer. It even works when the
   other phone is just sitting idle with the app closed — the tap wakes it into
   receive mode — and a single tap is all it takes.

   Technical: uses standard Android NFC. The receiver runs a Host Card Emulation
   (HCE) service on the Nearby Connections AID F00000FE2C; the sender uses NFC
   reader-mode. On tap the sender sends SELECT + an ADVERTISEMENT command:
   - If the other phone is actively visible as a receiver, its HCE answers with
     its endpoint info plus a Wi-Fi-LAN address (IP:port) packed in Quick Share's
     own NFC blob (the "deym" tag, marked P2P_POINT_TO_POINT), and the sender
     connects straight to that address.
   - If the other phone is idle/closed, the tap WAKES it instead: Android launches
     the receiver's HCE service even when its process is dead, which starts the
     receiver's foreground service, opens a short visibility window, and posts the
     Accept prompt. The sender treats the tap as a wake, opens a short window of
     its own, and lets the normal Wi-Fi discovery find the now-advertising
     receiver and auto-connect. This mirrors stock Quick Share, whose reader sends
     the ADVERTISEMENT once and relies on discovery rather than a blocking re-read.
   Either way NFC only does the handoff; the file transfer itself runs over the
   normal Wi-Fi (mDNS/TCP) path. The tap re-arms after each use, so repeated taps
   work.

   Android 15 routing: when two apps register the same NFC ID, Android 15 routes
   the tap to the default "wallet" app. So while our receive sheet is open the app
   claims the ID for the tap (CardEmulation.setPreferredService in
   ConsentTrampolineActivity) — the supported way to receive on that shared ID on
   Android 15 — and releases it when the sheet closes. On Android 14 the system
   shows a chooser instead.

   Note on the Accept prompt: between two phones signed in to the SAME Google
   account, stock Quick Share auto-accepts with no prompt (its self-share trust
   check). Across different accounts the receiver taps Accept once — the same as
   any cross-account Quick Share transfer.

   Status: send-by-tap is device-tested and starts the transfer; the Android 15
   receive-routing path is built and pending an on-device check. Full
   reverse-engineered byte map lives in docs/NFC_INTEROP_BYTEMAP.md.

4) The QR code button also turns your phone into an NFC tag for iPhones
   When you open the in-sheet QR code (the "Show QR" button on the send sheet),
   your phone simultaneously becomes a tappable NFC tag carrying that same pairing
   link. Tap an iPhone (or any phone) to the back and it opens the link in that
   phone's browser — a simple way to hand off to a device that doesn't run the app,
   no QR scanning needed.

   Technical: opening the QR panel publishes the freshly generated, single-use
   pairing URL (the exact link the QR encodes) to an NDEF Host Card Emulation
   service (SuperDropNdefApduService, AID D2760000850101). iOS reads NDEF tags
   without an app and offers to open the URL, so the iPhone lands on the pairing
   link in Safari. The QR button is the trigger: showing the QR sets the live URL
   and arms the tag; closing the QR / ending the send clears it. Because one NFC
   radio can't be a reader and a tag at once, opening the QR panel also drops the
   Quick Share tap reader-mode while the iPhone tag is armed. Separate from #3 —
   this is a plain NDEF URL tag, not the Quick Share AID exchange.

5) Made the heads-up notification prettier
   Redesigned the incoming-transfer pop-up so the Accept and Decline buttons are
   bigger, clearer, and easier to hit — color-coded and on opposite sides, with a
   thumbnail of what's coming in, so you can act on it at a glance.

   Technical: a custom RemoteViews notification layout under
   DecoratedCustomViewStyle (Decline left, Accept right) with a drawn placeholder
   thumbnail and a tinted small icon.

   Image for the PR: docs/pr-images/receive-notification.jpg

6) "Files received" notification with an Open button
   When a transfer finishes you get a heads-up that opens the file you just
   received in one tap (or your Downloads if it can't open it directly).

   Technical: a high-importance completion notification; the Open action resolves
   the received file's MediaStore Downloads content:// Uri (ACTION_VIEW with a
   read grant), falling back to the system Downloads view when the single file
   can't be resolved.

7) Automatically turn Wi-Fi and Bluetooth on for a transfer, then off again
   When a transfer starts, the app switches Wi-Fi and Bluetooth on for you and
   then puts them back exactly how they were once it's done, so you never have to
   fiddle with your radios. Set up once and it keeps working after every restart.
   (Requires a one-time wireless-debugging / ADB setup.)
   Ships with a small companion helper app that does the actual switching —
   needed because the main app can't toggle the radios on modern Android by
   itself; the helper handles it and the main app just asks it to.

   Technical:
   - Why a separate helper: the radio-toggle APIs are gated by targetSdk, not by
     a grantable permission. WifiManager.setWifiEnabled() only works for an app
     targeting SDK <= 28, and BluetoothAdapter.enable()/disable() only for SDK
     <= 32. The main app targets a modern SDK, so it legally can't call them; the
     helper is a tiny APK (dev.superdrop.radiohelper) that targets SDK 28 and can.
   - How apps talk to it: the helper exposes a bound Messenger service guarded by
     a signature permission (BIND_RADIO). Clients need the same signing key and a
     <queries> entry for the helper package. Two modes: SESSION
     (prepareForShare / transferFinished — the helper captures the original radio
     state, turns on only what was off, and restores only what it turned on) and
     DIRECT (setWifi / setBluetooth / queryState).
   - Bluetooth: zero setup — the SDK-28 helper calls BluetoothAdapter.enable()/
     disable() directly.
   - Wi-Fi (silent): needs WRITE_SECURE_SETTINGS, which a sideloaded app can only
     get over ADB. The helper does this once, on-device, with no PC needed after
     setup:
       * pair to the phone's own wireless debugging (Android 11+) via a
         notification inline-reply (so the pairing dialog stays open), then
       * self-grant the permission:
           adb shell pm grant dev.superdrop.radiohelper android.permission.WRITE_SECURE_SETTINGS
         (run once; survives reboot, lost only on reinstall), then
       * toggle Wi-Fi over the local ADB connection with:
           svc wifi enable   /   svc wifi disable
   - No per-reboot step: on boot the helper re-enables wireless debugging
     (adb_wifi_enabled via WRITE_SECURE_SETTINGS), re-finds the local adbd port
     over mDNS (_adb-tls-connect._tcp), and reconnects with its stored key.
   - Fallback ladder if a silent path isn't available on the device: direct
     setWifiEnabled -> self-ADB -> Shizuku -> the Wi-Fi settings panel.

8) Battery-optimization button that actually works on OnePlus
   The built-in "stop the app being put to sleep" button didn't work on OnePlus,
   so we replaced it with a one-tap Allow that sticks (and falls back to the
   settings list on phones that ignore the pop-up).

   Technical: fires ACTION_REQUEST_IGNORE_BATTERY_OPTIMIZATIONS (the one-tap
   "Allow" dialog) and, on OEM ROMs that no-op it, escalates to the App Info /
   battery settings page.

9) Cleaner device names in the send picker (no raw IP addresses)
   Nearby devices that don't broadcast a name — typically a stock or "hidden"
   Quick Share phone — now show a friendly "Quick Share device" label in the send
   sheet instead of their raw IP address, so the picker never shows cryptic numbers.

   Technical: the picker label falls back through BLE name → "Quick Share device
   (endpointId)" → "Quick Share BLE device"; the final fallback used to return the
   internal stableId (a LAN address), which leaked an IP into the UI. It now returns
   a generic "Quick Share device" label instead. (Two Super Drop phones advertise
   with their real names, so this only affects nameless/hidden peers.)

15) Groundwork / research for Google's upcoming "Tap to Share"
    Google is building an AirDrop-style "Tap to Share" into Android (tap two phones
    to share files/contacts). It isn't released yet. We reverse-engineered the
    in-progress code inside Google Play Services to see how it works and whether we
    could match it, and wrote up the findings so we have a head start for when Google
    ships it. This item is research and a written plan only — there is NO new code
    wired into the app, and nothing here turns on by itself when Google releases.

    What we found (the short version):
    - Google's tap is just a connection HANDOFF that then transfers over normal
      Nearby/Wi-Fi — the SAME transport this app already uses for Quick Share. The
      NFC side is plain Android Host Card Emulation, and there is NO Google-account /
      attestation lock on the handshake. So in principle it's a feature we could add.
    - The catch: the exact over-the-wire bytes (the NFC ID it answers on and the
      handoff record it sends) live in a part of Google Play Services that Google only
      ships to phones with the feature switched on. A normal app download — even the
      newest developer build — doesn't include them. So we can't make it talk to a
      real Google phone until either Google releases it or we pull that piece off a
      device that has it enabled.

    Why not just ship "preliminary" stubs now: copying Google's service/permission
    names into our app would do nothing — those belong to Google Play Services, a
    third-party app can't host that feature, and Google's release does not look for or
    light up our stubs. So that would be dead code mislabeled as a feature, which we
    deliberately did not add.

    What we CAN build without Google (a future item, not done here): our own
    tap-to-share between two Super Drop phones, reusing the tap + Nearby/Wi-Fi code we
    already have (#3) plus a small handoff record of our own. That's ~two-thirds reuse,
    one-third new — but it would be OUR feature, not interop with Google, and must not
    be labelled "works with Google Tap to Share" until that's actually tested.

    Full write-up: docs/research/google-tap-to-share-gesture-exchange.md
    Status: research / groundwork only. Revisit when Google ships it to a stable
    release AND we have an NFC device with the feature enabled.

16) Send to Windows: fix "Can't complete transfer" (issue #200)
    Sending a file from the phone to Windows Quick Share showed "Sent" on the phone but
    "Can't complete transfer" on Windows — even though the whole file actually arrived.
    Phone-to-phone and Windows-to-phone already worked; only phone-to-Windows failed.

    Cause: right after sending the last byte, our app immediately told the receiver to
    disconnect. Windows treats a disconnect that lands before it has finished writing the
    file as a failure, so it threw the completed transfer away. (Android receivers are
    more forgiving and kept the file — which is why phone-to-phone worked.) The real
    Quick Share sender doesn't hang up early; it leaves the connection open and lets the
    receiver finish and close first.

    Fix: do what Quick Share does, but only for the receivers that need it. Every receiver
    advertises whether it supports the "safe to disconnect" handshake. Samsung, Android,
    and our own app DO (and are completely unaffected — same behavior as before). Windows
    says it does NOT. For a receiver that doesn't, we no longer send the early disconnect:
    we wait for it to finish and close, then close (with a generous timeout as a safety
    net). The file already transferred, so this only changes WHEN we hang up.

    Risk: low and well-contained — phone-to-phone and the receiving direction are
    untouched; only the "send to a Windows-style receiver" path changed. The detected
    receiver type is logged.

    Status: built; needs a real Windows Quick Share receiver to confirm end-to-end (can't
    test Windows interop in the build env). A phone-to-phone send is a no-regression check.
    Code: core-protocol OutboundConnectionDriver (gate the terminal disconnect on the
    receiver's advertised safe_to_disconnect_version).
